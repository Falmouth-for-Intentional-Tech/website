## What the research says

When Los Angeles Unified wrote its new screen time policy, its Office of Research and Program Evaluation [reviewed the evidence](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1aUGrwgNFKvCq3CBmO3dzCC3kKnOenpx3/view). Their key findings:

- **Handing out devices doesn't raise achievement on its own.** Benefits show up when technology is built into teacher-led instruction, not when it replaces the teacher. Programs that substitute for teacher interaction show null or mixed results.
- **Younger children are the most vulnerable.** Unsupervised or passive screen time carries more risk for preschool and early elementary students, and having an adult involved helps protect language and self-regulation.
- **Distraction is built into the platforms.** Autoplay, algorithmic feeds and multitasking are linked to weaker attention and can undercut the learning a device is meant to support.
- **School screen time adds up.** There's no exact safe limit, but total daily screen time above about two hours is associated with poorer attention, sleep and well-being. Time on school devices and homework counts toward that total.
- **Take-home devices raise equity concerns.** Families differ in how closely they can supervise home use, so unrestricted take-home devices can widen gaps between students.

The review concludes that rules should be strictest for the youngest students and loosen gradually with age, and that districts should keep measuring what their technology actually does for students.

## What's happening in our schools

Here's what Falmouth Public Schools says publicly about classroom technology:

- **The district's starting point.** The [Technology department's mission](https://www.falmouth.k12.ma.us/departments/technology) says that technology "not only serves but elevates learning."
- **Technology woven into daily lessons.** The district's [Instructional Technology page](https://www.falmouth.k12.ma.us/academics/digital_learning/digital_learning/instructional_technology) aims for technology use that is "routine and transparent" and always available for the task at hand. At its best, the page says, using technology should be "second nature" for students and teachers. Teachers are pointed to the SAMR model for planning technology-integrated lessons.
- **1:1 Chromebooks since 2015.** Falmouth piloted a 1:1 Chromebook program at Morse Pond School in 2014–15 and expanded it to Grade 5. A state Digital Connections Partnership Grant paid for wireless upgrades, and the district moved to a full 1:1 Chromebook program in the 2015–16 school year ([Digital Learning page](https://www.falmouth.k12.ma.us/academics/digital_learning/digital_learning)).
- **Google accounts for every student.** Students get district-managed Google Workspace (G Suite) accounts for email, file storage and shared documents, which they can use from any internet-connected device.
- **Digital citizenship lessons.** Since fall 2019, students in grades K–8 have had [digital citizenship lessons](https://www.falmouth.k12.ma.us/academics/digital_learning/digital_learning/digital_citizenship) from Common Sense Media's curriculum. The lessons cover six themes, including media balance and well-being, privacy, and cyberbullying. In spring 2020, Falmouth became the first Massachusetts district to earn Common Sense District Recognition. Students can also take a self-paced "Tech Driver's Ed" course and test to earn a "Digital Driver's License."
- **Recommended websites for students.** The Digital Learning department [lists dozens of websites](https://www.falmouth.k12.ma.us/cms/One.aspx?portalId=6135966&pageId=7403846) for students, starting in kindergarten. They include reading and audiobook sites, typing practice from grade K, coding sites, digital art tools and movement videos such as GoNoodle, with a separate list for upper elementary and middle school.


**The district's website doesn't currently say how much daily screen time students have, or which sites and apps are blocked. Those are among the questions below.**

### How this compares with the evidence

Falmouth's stated goal is technology that "elevates learning," used routinely and readily available until it's "second nature." The [research LAUSD reviewed](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1aUGrwgNFKvCq3CBmO3dzCC3kKnOenpx3/view) points the other way:

| Falmouth's approach | What the evidence suggests |
| --- | --- |
| Technology "elevates learning." ([Technology dept.](https://www.falmouth.k12.ma.us/departments/technology)) | Handing out devices rarely improves achievement on its own. Gains are modest, and appear only when technology supports teacher-led instruction. (Fairlie & Robinson, 2013; Pane et al., 2015; Roschelle et al., 2016; Zheng et al., 2016) |
| Technology should be "routine" and "readily available for the task at hand." ([Instructional Technology](https://www.falmouth.k12.ma.us/academics/digital_learning/digital_learning/instructional_technology)) | Screen time adds up across the school day and homework. More than about two hours a day in total is linked to poorer attention, sleep and well-being. (Twenge & Campbell, 2018; Sanders et al., 2019; Leonhardt et al., 2025) |
| Using technology should be "second nature" for students. ([Instructional Technology](https://www.falmouth.k12.ma.us/academics/digital_learning/digital_learning/instructional_technology)) | Use should be limited and purposeful, with the strictest limits for the youngest students. (Madigan et al., 2020; Leonhardt et al., 2025) |
| Recommended websites and typing practice begin in kindergarten. ([Digital Learning](https://www.falmouth.k12.ma.us/cms/One.aspx?portalId=6135966&pageId=7403846)) | Young children are the most vulnerable to unsupervised or passive screen time. (Madigan et al., 2020; Cerniglia et al., 2021) |

**We aren't asking Falmouth to give up technology. We're asking the district to start from the evidence: use screens where they clearly help, and limit them everywhere else, especially in the early grades.**

## Questions we're asking

- What is each platform used for, and what's the evidence it helps?
- How much daily screen time do students have at each grade?
- Which sites and apps are blocked on student devices?
- What are the phone policies, and how are they enforced?

## What we're advocating for

These recommendations are adapted from [Schools Beyond Screens](https://schoolsbeyondscreens.com/reform-proposals).

- Eliminate all device use for TK–2.
- Eliminate 1:1 device use for grades 3–8, with limited device use through a computer cart or lab model where it is appropriate for the curriculum.
- Block student access to YouTube, as well as all gaming, video-streaming and social media, on student devices.
- Require all apps used for instruction or on school-issued devices to be ad-free.
- Disable student access to any generative AI tools, apps, websites and software on all school-issued devices until this technology has been proven safe, legal and effective, and until age-appropriate guidelines are established.
- Prohibit all student device use during passing periods, lunch and recess.
- Require explicit parental consent for each app that collects data, per FTC guidelines on COPPA.
- Be transparent about technology use in schools, both on devices and in teacher-led instruction.
- Create clear pathways for parents and students who wish to opt out of device-based instruction, without penalty or reprisal, and provide low-tech alternatives.
- Require all EdTech products to meet independent standards for efficacy, and to prove that any benefits outweigh potential risks.
- Prohibit screen time from being used as a reward, incentive or behavioral reinforcement for students.

## What other districts are doing

**Cambridge, MA.** Cambridge Public Schools has [published grade-by-grade limits](https://www.cpsd.us/administration/information-communication-technology-services/healthy-habits-and-tech-limits). Students in PreK–2 generally don't have individual screen-based devices, and grades 3–5 have only limited access. Screens are generally not allowed at lunch or recess through 8th grade, and devices aren't used as rewards or free-time activities. Upper grades build in off-screen breaks every 20 to 30 minutes, and the district is adding evening cutoff times for digital homework.

**Los Angeles Unified, CA.** The country's second-largest district adopted a [Student Screen Time Policy](https://edtech.lausd.org/apps/pages/screentime) built on the research above. It blocks student access to YouTube, social media, streaming and non-approved games. It limits take-home devices before high school and makes them opt-in for families. It also gives parents the right to see how much time their child spends on devices, and asks teachers to put non-screen homework first.

**Worcester, MA.** Parents petitioned to remove individually assigned devices from preschool through 2nd grade. On November 12, 2026, the school committee [sent the petition for further review](https://rock929rocks.com/2026/11/16/worcester-parents-say-they-want-less-screen-time-for-students-in-early-school-grades/) by its teaching and learning subcommittee.

**Boston, MA.** Parents organized by Schools Beyond Screens are [petitioning the Boston School Committee](https://jamaicaplainnews.com/2026/10/05/petition-implores-boston-school-committee-to-set-student-screen-time-policy/691798) for a district-wide screen time policy. It would remove devices from early education through 2nd grade, move grades 3–8 to shared carts and labs, and set grade-level limits.
